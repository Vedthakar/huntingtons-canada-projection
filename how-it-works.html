How the model works

The projection is a deterministic age-structured cohort model. It takes a population of expansion carriers, ages it one year at a time for fifty years, and applies the Mendelian transmission rule to every conception. Nothing is fitted and nothing is learned from data — the behaviour comes entirely from the biology written into it.

The engine

One year of the simulation

Everything the model does is this loop, run 50 times. The population of carriers sits on the left as 101 age cohorts. Each year they reproduce, their children inherit or don't, the policy intercepts some of those conceptions, new mutations arrive from outside, and everyone gets a year older.

κ is the share of at-risk conceptions the policy reaches; ε is per-embryo efficacy. The policy filter sits on the inherited stream only — which is why the de novo box is drawn outside it.

The finding that matters

Why the two lines barely separate before 2070

This is the single most important thing to understand before presenting. A prevented conception is not a prevented case for about forty-five years. The chart's window ends in 2076, so most of the benefit of a 2035 policy lands beyond the right-hand edge of the graph.

Switching the tool to All carriers removes the onset gate and shows the effect roughly 40 years earlier. That view is the honest way to demonstrate that the policy is doing something.

Say this before anyone asks it. If a judge spots the flat lines before you explain them, it reads as a failed model. If you explain the lag first, it reads as the strongest point in the argument: germline intervention is a generational investment, and the Act is being asked to authorise something whose benefit arrives after everyone in the room is gone. That reframes the whole debate away from near-term utility and toward intergenerational duty.

The three curves

Everything the model knows about biology

These are plotted live from the model file, so they are exactly the numbers the projection runs on. Each is a published epidemiological schedule, not a fitted parameter.

Onset — F(a)

Cumulative probability that a full-penetrance carrier has converted to manifest disease. Logistic, median 45. This curve is the source of the lag.

Mortality — m(a)

Background annual death probability, Gompertz. Carriers additionally take an excess hazard of 0.055/yr once symptomatic, giving ~18 years median survival after onset.

Fertility — f(a)

Children per person-year, both sexes, peaking at 30 and summing to a TFR of 1.26. This is what determines how fast the carrier pool replaces itself.

Calibration

Why the baseline is exactly flat

A projection is only useful if the counterfactual is clean. The model is calibrated so that, with no policy, prevalence per capita holds exactly constant at the observed rate — so every change in the orange line is attributable to the intervention and nothing else. Absolute case counts still rise, purely because the population rises, which is the comparison the case brief asks for.

K = (prevalence / 100,000) × population2026 ÷ Σa w[a]·π·F(a) // total carriers in 2026
birthsrequired = K · w[0] · (1 + g) // to hold per-capita flat
birthsde novo = birthsrequired × φ // φ = de novo share
birthsinherited = birthsrequired − birthsde novo // the stream policy acts on

The fertility scale is then back-solved so the age structure reproduces that inherited figure. Verified numerically: the baseline holds 13.70 per 100,000 in both 2026 and 2076, with the absolute count rising 5,686 → 7,668 as the population grows 41.5M → 56.0M.

A question that will come up

This is not a machine learning model, deliberately

Machine learning finds patterns in training data. There is no training data here. Canada has roughly one reliable prevalence estimate for Huntington's disease and no longitudinal registry of carrier conceptions, so there is nothing for a learning algorithm to learn from — and a model fitted to a single point, extrapolated fifty years, would be indefensible under any scrutiny.

What the problem calls for instead is a mechanistic model: the generating process is known exactly. Autosomal dominant inheritance is 0.5 per conception. Onset follows a measured distribution. Populations age at measured rates. When you know the mechanism, you encode it rather than trying to infer it.

This matters in the room. A mechanistic model is fully auditable — every coefficient traces to a citation, and a judge can challenge any one of them by moving a slider and watching the conclusion hold or break. A learned model would be a black box fitted to data that does not exist. If asked whether this is AI or machine learning, the correct answer is no, and the reason is a strength.

What a judge might askThe answer
"Did you train this?"No. It is a compartmental cohort model. Every parameter is published and cited; nothing is estimated from data we collected.
"Where's your confidence interval?"There isn't one, because it is deterministic. Uncertainty is handled by exposing every input as a slider so you can test any assumption live.
"Isn't 50 years too far to project?"Yes, as a forecast. It isn't one. It is a comparison of two scenarios under identical assumptions, where the assumptions cancel and only the policy differs.
"Why no ancestry term?"Transmission is 0.5 regardless of ancestry. The prevalence gap between populations is a founder effect in haplotype frequency, not a difference in inheritance.

Limits

What this model cannot tell you

  • It cannot price the intervention. No cost, no QALYs, no budget impact. If the panel asks about cost-effectiveness, that is outside the model.
  • It assumes coverage is a single number. In reality uptake would vary by region, income and family awareness, and the people hardest to reach are the ones least likely to have been tested.
  • It treats efficacy as constant over 50 years. Realistically it would improve. Holding it flat is the conservative choice.
  • It ignores anticipation. CAG repeats tend to lengthen across generations, especially on paternal transmission, which shifts onset earlier in successive generations. Including it would slightly worsen the no-policy line.
  • It does not model reduced-penetrance alleles separately. The 36–39 CAG band is folded into a single penetrance constant of 0.95.
  • It says nothing about whether the policy is right. It quantifies one consequence. The ethical argument is a separate job.

Naming these yourself is worth more than hoping nobody asks. A team that states its own limits reads as competent; a team that gets them extracted in Q&A reads as having oversold.
